# United Kingdom — legal source record (UK-C)

This file is the source record for the UK legal pack (phase 4, UK-C in `docs/markets/PLAN.md`; market `gb`, brand
Empvibe, X-111). Every Norwegian legal reference in the product's texts needs a UK counterpart. Every counterpart below
was read in a primary source fetched on **10 October 2026**: legislation.gov.uk (revised texts), hse.gov.uk, hseni.gov.uk,
ico.org.uk, gov.uk (HMRC, Companies House) and developer.company-information.service.gov.uk. The licence text was read on
nationalarchives.gov.uk. Quotes are verbatim, including the statutes' own words («he», «his»). Where the UK has no
counterpart, the section says so. Anything that could not be confirmed from a fetched source is marked **UNVERIFIED** and
listed at the end. Lines marked **Assessment** are my own reading, not a statement by the source. This is groundwork for
legal review, not legal advice.

Citation style used throughout: «HSWA 1974 s.2(3)», «MHSWR 1999 reg 3(6)», «SRSC 1977 reg 9», «HSCER 1996 reg 4(1)(a)»,
«UK GDPR art. 6(1)(c)», «DPA 2018 s.164A», «VATA 1994 s.8». For HSE guidance, cite the page title and URL. Guidance is
never cited as law.

## Jurisdiction: Great Britain and Northern Ireland are separate

Health and safety law is not UK-wide. The Act says so itself, in **HSWA 1974 s.84(1)**: «This Act, except— (a) Part I and
this Part so far as may be necessary to enable regulations under section 15 … to be made and operate for the purpose
mentioned in paragraph 2 of Schedule 3; and (b) paragraphs . . . 3 of Schedule 9, does not extend to Northern Ireland.»
(The omitted words, «or 30», are marked as repealed for England, Wales and Scotland.)
The MHSWR 1999 and the HSCER 1996 carry the extent marker «E+W+S» on legislation.gov.uk. Northern Ireland has its own
instruments, named in sections 2 and 5. They are enforced by HSENI. Data protection, VAT and Companies House are
UK-wide. The Equality Act 2010 provisions cited in section 4 carry «E+W+S», and Northern Ireland's equality law was not
researched.

A UK product text that cites the HSWA or the MHSWR is therefore citing Great Britain law. It must either name the
Northern Ireland twin as well or say «Great Britain».

Three counting rules matter, and product texts must not mix them:
- MHSWR 1999 regs 3(6) and 5(2) count the employer's **employees**: «Where the employer employs five or more employees».
- The written health and safety policy (HSWA 1974 s.2(3)) is excepted per **undertaking**: «Any employer who carries on an
  undertaking in which for the time being he employs less than five employees is hereby excepted as respects that
  undertaking» (1975 Exception Regs reg 2).
- **Representation and committees have no headcount threshold at all** (section 5).

HSE's plain-English pages say «workers» («If you have five or more workers, you are required by law to write the risk
assessment down»), but the regulation says «employees». Product texts should follow the regulation.

The prescribed cases for s.2(4) and s.2(7) are in SRSC 1977 regs 3 and 9 (section 5).

**Definitions, s.53(1)**, as relevant to a psychosocial product:
- «“personal injury” includes any disease and any impairment of a person’s physical or mental condition;». Mental
  impairment is within the Act's concept of injury. This is the statutory anchor for treating psychosocial risk as a
  health risk.
- «“employee” means an individual who works under a contract of employment [or is treated by section 51A as being an
  employee], and related expressions shall be construed accordingly;»

**Enforcement** (relevant to the product's promise of the documentation an inspector asks for):
- s.20(2)(k): an inspector may «require the production of, inspect, and take copies of or of any entry in— (i) any
  books or documents which by virtue of any of the relevant statutory provisions are required to be kept; and (ii) any
  other books or documents which it is necessary for him to see for the purposes of any examination or investigation
  under paragraph (d) above;».
- s.21 (improvement notice): «If an inspector is of the opinion that a person— (a) is contravening one or more of the
  relevant statutory provisions; or (b) has contravened one or more of those provisions in circumstances that make it
  likely that the contravention will continue or be repeated, he may serve on him a notice (in this Part referred to as
  “an improvement notice”) …».
- s.37(1), officers' liability: «Where an offence under any of the relevant statutory provisions committed by a body
  corporate is proved to have been committed with the consent or connivance of, or to have been attributable to any
  neglect on the part of, any director, manager, secretary or other similar officer of the body corporate or a person
  who was purporting to act in any such capacity, he as well as the body corporate shall be guilty of that offence …».
- Inspectors come from HSE **or the local authority**. From HSE's «What the law says about consulting workers»: «health and
  safety inspectors (from HSE and local authorities) can enforce for failure to comply with legal duties on procedural
  matters.» Which authority enforces at which premises is set by other regulations, which were not read
  (UNVERIFIED 15).

**Northern Ireland:** HSW (NI) Order 1978 **art. 4** has substantively the same wording («Regulations may provide» in
place of «Regulations made by the Secretary of State may provide»), with different paragraph numbers: art. 4(1)
general duty, 4(3) written policy, 4(4) safety representatives, **4(5)** duty to consult, **4(6)** safety committee.
Art. 4(1): «It shall be the duty of every employer to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health, safety
and welfare at work of all his employees.»

**How a product text should cite it:** «HSWA 1974 s.2(1) (in Northern Ireland, art. 4(1) of the Health and Safety at Work
(Northern Ireland) Order 1978)»; written policy «HSWA 1974 s.2(3); in writing from five employees (SI 1975/1584)»;
consultation «HSWA 1974 s.2(6)». Write «so far as is reasonably practicable» in full. It is the standard of the duty,
and the duty is not absolute.

«The preventive and protective measures» is defined in reg 1(2): «the measures which have been identified by the
employer or by the self-employed person in consequence of the assessment as the measures he needs to take to comply with
the requirements and prohibitions imposed upon him by or under the relevant statutory provisions».

**Groups especially at risk:** reg 3(6)(b) requires them to be *recorded* where the assessment identifies them. The MHSWR
names particular groups elsewhere: young persons (reg 3(4)–(5)) and new or expectant mothers (reg 1(2) definition). For
the product, a factor result for a group (department, role) that the employer judges «especially at risk» is what
reg 3(6)(b) asks to be written down.

HSE's plain-English statements, which agree with the regulations:
- «Under the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, the minimum you must do is: identify what could
  cause injury or illness in your business (hazards); decide how likely it is that someone could be harmed and how
  seriously (the risk); take action to eliminate the hazard, or if this isn't possible, control the risk» (HSE, «Managing
  risks and risk assessment at work»).
- «If you have five or more employees, you must write your policy down. If you have fewer than five employees you do
  not have to write anything down, but it is useful to do so.» (HSE, «Prepare a health and safety policy»).
- «As an employer, you must appoint a competent person or people to help you meet your health and safety legal duties.»
  and «You could appoint (one or a combination of): yourself; one or more of your workers; someone from outside your
  business» (HSE, «Appoint a competent person»).

**Northern Ireland:** MHSWR (NI) 2000 (SR 2000/388), «in operation at 1.2.2001». Reg 3(6) and reg 5(1)–(2) read word for
word as in Great Britain (verified). Reg 7 is headed «Health and safety assistance» and reg 10 «Information for
employees», with the same opening words (verified, first paragraphs only).

**How a product text should cite it:** risk assessment «MHSWR 1999 reg 3 (Northern Ireland: MHSWR (NI) 2000 reg 3)»; the
written record «significant findings and any group especially at risk, recorded from five employees (MHSWR 1999
reg 3(6))»; plan and follow-up «arrangements for planning, organisation, control, monitoring and review (MHSWR 1999
reg 5), recorded from five employees (reg 5(2))»; information to staff «MHSWR 1999 reg 10». Do not call the record a
«report» to anyone. It is kept by the employer and produced to an inspector on request (HSWA 1974 s.20(2)(k)).

## 3. Work-related stress: HSE's Management Standards and the HSENI statement

**Norway:** aml § 4-3 and forskrift om utførelse av arbeid kap. 1A, which name the psychosocial factors.

**Great Britain: no statute or regulation names psychosocial factors.** The duty is the general risk assessment duty in
section 2. HSE's guidance applies it to stress.

**HSE: the legal duty, in HSE's words**
- «Employers have a legal duty to protect workers from stress at work by doing a risk assessment and acting on it. This
  is the same duty you have to protect people from other health and safety risks.» (HSE, «Work-related stress and how to

## 5. Representation: safety representatives, elected representatives, or none

**Norway:** aml §§ 6-1, 6-2 (verneombud) and §§ 7-1, 7-2 (AMU). The product shows «§ 6-2 safety representative
involved».

**Great Britain: two regimes, chosen by whether a recognised trade union has appointed representatives.**

*Union-appointed safety representatives — SRSC 1977*
- **Appointment, reg 3(1):** «For the purposes of section 2(4) of the 1974 Act, a recognised trade union may appoint
  safety representatives from amongst the employees in all cases where one or more employees are employed by an
  employer by whom it is recognised ….» The trigger is union recognition. The headcount is «one or more».
- Notification, reg 3(2): «Where the employer has been notified in writing by or on behalf of a trade union of the names
  of the persons appointed as safety representatives under this Regulation and the group or groups of employees they
  represent, each such safety representative shall have the functions set out in Regulation 4 below.»
- Experience, reg 3(4): «A person appointed under paragraph (1) above as a safety representative shall so far as is
  reasonably practicable either have been employed by his employer throughout the preceding two years or have had at
  least two years experience in similar employment.»
- «Recognised trade union» (reg 2(1)) is an independent trade union «which the employer concerned recognises for the
  purpose of negotiations relating to or connected with one or more of the matters specified in section 29(1) of that
  Act …».
- **Functions, reg 4(1)** include «(a) to investigate potential hazards and dangerous occurrences at the workplace …»,
  «(b) to investigate complaints by any employee he represents relating to that employee's health, safety or welfare at
  work;», «(d) to make representations to the employer on general matters affecting the health, safety or welfare at work
  of the employees at the workplace;», «(f) to represent the employees … in consultations at the workplace with inspectors
  …», and «(h) to attend meetings of safety committees …». It ends: «no function given to a safety representative by this
  paragraph shall be construed as imposing any duty on him.»
- **Consultation in good time, reg 4A(1):** «every employer shall consult safety representatives in good time with regard
  to— (a) the introduction of any measure at the workplace which may substantially affect the health and safety of the
  employees the safety representatives concerned represent; (b) his arrangements for appointing or, as the case may be,
  nominating persons in accordance with regulations 7(1) and 8(1)(b) of the Management of Health and Safety at Work
  Regulations 1999 …; (c) any health and safety information he is required to provide to the employees …; (d) the
  planning and organisation of any health and safety training …; and (e) the health and safety consequences for the
  employees … of the introduction (including the planning thereof) of new technologies into the workplace.»
- **Documents and information, reg 7:** reg 7(1) allows inspection and copies of documents the employer must keep, «except
  a document consisting of or relating to any health record of an identifiable individual». Reg 7(2) requires the
  employer to make available «the information, within the employer's knowledge, necessary to enable them to fulfil their
  functions» except, among others, «(c) any information relating specifically to an individual, unless he has consented
  to its being disclosed;». This matches the product's rule that representatives see aggregates and never individual
  comments.
- **Safety committee, reg 9(1):** «For the purposes of section 2(7) of the 1974 Act … the prescribed cases shall be any
  cases in which at least two safety representatives request the employer in writing to establish a safety committee.»
  Reg 9(2): the employer «(a) … shall consult with the safety representatives who made the request and with the
  representatives of recognised trade unions …; (b) … shall post a notice stating the composition of the committee and
  the workplace or workplaces to be covered by it …; (c) the committee shall be established not later than three months
  after the request for it.»

*Employees not represented by union safety representatives — HSCER 1996*
- **Duty, reg 3:** «Where there are employees who are not represented by safety representatives under the 1977
  Regulations, the employer shall consult those employees in good time on matters relating to their health and safety at
  work and, in particular, with regard to— (a) the introduction of any measure at the workplace which may substantially
  affect the health and safety of those employees; …» (then (b)–(e) as in SRSC reg 4A(1)).
- **Direct or elected, reg 4(1):** «The consultation required by regulation 3 is consultation with either— (a) the
  employees directly; or (b) in respect of any group of employees, one or more persons in that group who were elected,
  by the employees in that group at the time of the election, to represent that group for the purposes of such
  consultation (and any such persons are in these Regulations referred to as “representatives of employee safety”).»
- Reg 4(2): where representatives are consulted, the employer «shall inform the employees represented by those
  representatives of— (a) the names of those representatives; and (b) the group of employees represented by those
  representatives.» Reg 4(4): «Where an employer who has been consulting representatives of employee safety decides to
  consult employees directly he shall inform the employees and the representatives of that fact.»
- Information, reg 5(1): «Where an employer consults employees directly he shall, subject to paragraph (3), make
  available to those employees such information, within the employer’s knowledge, as is necessary to enable them to
  participate fully and effectively in the consultation.» Reg 5(3)(c) excludes information «relating specifically to an
  individual, unless he has consented to its being disclosed».
- Functions, reg 6: representations on hazards and «general matters affecting the health and safety at work of the group
  of employees he represents», and representing the group with inspectors. Reg 7: training, paid time off and
  facilities.
- **No committee route.** Regs 3–8 of the HSCER contain no safety committee provision. A committee arises only under SRSC
  reg 9.

*HSE's summary* («What the law says about consulting workers», updated 2026-01-15):
- «If you recognise trade unions in any part of the business: the Safety Representatives and Safety Committees
  Regulations 1977 will apply; the trade union may appoint health and safety representatives (referred to as safety
  representatives in the Regulations); you must consult the union-appointed health and safety representatives on health
  and safety matters affecting the employees they represent.»
- «Where employees are not represented under the Safety Representatives and Safety Committees Regulations 1977, the
  Health and Safety (Consultation with Employees) Regulations 1996 will apply.»
- **«You can choose to consult employees directly as individuals, or through elected health and safety representatives
  (known as ‘representatives of employee safety’ in the Regulations), or a combination of the two.»**
- «If you have a small business, or you have regular contact with all your employees, consulting with individuals is
  often effective.»
- «If 2 or more union health and safety representatives ask in writing for a health and safety committee, you must set
  one up within 3 months.»
- «The presence of a union health and safety representative does not prevent managers from communicating directly with
  the workforce as a whole.»
- «Where you already have existing consultation arrangements that satisfy health and safety law, you do not have to
  change them.»

HSE, «Consult your workers»: «You must consult all your employees on health and safety.» · «In a small business, you might
choose to consult your workers directly. Larger businesses may consult through a health and safety representative, chosen
by your employees or selected by a trade union. **As an employer, you cannot decide who the representative will be.**»

**Plainly stated:**
1. **A UK employer may lawfully have no safety representative.** If no recognised trade union has appointed safety
   representatives, and the employees have not elected representatives of employee safety, the employer consults the
   employees **directly** (HSCER 1996 reg 4(1)(a)). The duty to consult always applies (HSWA 1974 s.2(6) / HSCER reg 3).
   Having a representative is not required.
2. **There is no headcount threshold**, five or any other, for having a representative or for the duty to consult.
   SRSC reg 3(1) applies «where one or more employees are employed». HSCER reg 3 applies «Where there are employees who
   are not represented». Neither has a minimum (confirmed in the texts read).
3. **There is no headcount threshold for a safety committee either.** One is required only when at least two union safety
   representatives ask in writing (SRSC reg 9(1)), within three months (reg 9(2)(c)). A workforce with no recognised union
   has no route to a mandatory committee.
4. **The employer does not appoint the representative.** A union appoints safety representatives, and employees elect
   representatives of employee safety (HSCER reg 4(1)(b): «elected, by the employees in that group»).
5. Protection: ERA 1996 s.44(1)(b) and (ba) protect representatives, committee members and anyone who «took part (or
   proposed to take part) in consultation with the employer pursuant to the Health and Safety (Consultation with
   Employees) Regulations 1996 or in an election of representatives of employee safety».

**Northern Ireland:** SRSC (NI) 1979 (SR 1979/437), https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/1979/437/contents/made, published only
as a PDF of the made text, which was not read. HSCER (NI) 1996 (SR 1996/511) reg 3 reads «Where there are employees who are
not represented by safety representatives under the 1979 Regulations, the employer shall consult those employees in good
time …», and reg 4(1) gives the same choice («(a) the employees directly; or (b) … “representatives of employee safety”»).
Verified on the made text.

**How a product text should cite it:** «union-appointed safety representative (SRSC 1977 reg 3)»; «elected representative of
employee safety (HSCER 1996 reg 4)»; «where there is no representative, the employer consults employees directly (HSCER
1996 reg 4(1)(a))»; «safety committee when two union safety representatives ask in writing (SRSC 1977 reg 9)». For the
«consulted before the survey» marker: «consulted in good time (SRSC 1977 reg 4A / HSCER 1996 reg 3)». Whether a survey
is «a measure … which may substantially affect» health and safety is an interpretation (UNVERIFIED 10). Present the marker
as good practice that the duty to consult supports, not as a statutory step.

### 6.2 Lawful basis in employment (ICO)

The ICO's employment guidance does not use the phrase «consent is rarely appropriate» (searched across all pages fetched;
not found). Its actual words:
- «A person must freely give their consent for it to be valid. This means that **consent is not usually appropriate in the
  employment context**, due to the imbalance of power between you and your workers. Workers are likely to feel that they
  have no choice but to give you consent.» (ICO, «Data protection and monitoring workers»).
- «It may be difficult for you to rely on consent to keep records of personal information about your workers. This is
  because, as an employer, you will generally be in a position of power over your workers. They may fear adverse
  consequences and may feel they have no choice but to agree to you collecting and using their information. In such
  circumstances, consent is not considered freely given.» · «You should avoid relying on consent unless you are confident
  you can demonstrate it is freely given.» · «If you think it will be difficult for you to show that consent has been
  freely given, you should consider relying on a different lawful basis, such as legitimate interests.» (ICO, «Collecting
  and keeping employment records»).
- «Consent will not usually be appropriate if there is a clear imbalance of power between you and the individual. … This
  will be a particular issue for public authorities and employers.» · «However, public authorities and employers are not
  banned from using consent as their lawful basis.» (ICO, «When is consent appropriate?»).
- Legal obligation: «You could rely on this lawful basis where you need to use personal information kept in employment
  records to comply with a common law or statutory obligation (this does not include contractual obligations).» Legitimate
  interests: «As part of this, you should carry out a legitimate interests assessment to determine if this is the case.»
  (ICO, «Collecting and keeping employment records»).
- Special category (employment condition): «To rely on this condition for keeping employment records, you must be keeping
  them to comply with employment law, or social security and social protection law. You should identify the legal
  obligation or right, either by referring to the specific legal provision or by pointing to an appropriate source of
  advice or guidance that sets it out clearly.» and «If you are relying on this condition, you must also meet the
  associated condition in Part 1 of Schedule 1 of the DPA 2018. This condition also requires you to have an appropriate
  policy document in place.» (same page).
- Health information includes «a questionnaire completed by workers to detect problems with their health» (ICO, «Data
  protection and workers' health information»).

**Assessment** (mine, not an ICO statement): the employer is the controller and Orgpuls AS is its processor. For a
private employer, the defensible basis for a work-environment survey is UK GDPR art. 6(1)(c), with MHSWR 1999 reg 3 and
HSWA 1974 s.2 as the obligation, and art. 6(1)(f) as the fallback. The regulations require an assessment, not a survey in
particular, so «necessary» is a judgement, as in Sweden. A public authority would use art. 6(1)(e). The Orgpuls core
items ask about work conditions, not health. If a module or free-text field could reveal health at an individual level,
art. 9(2)(b) with DPA 2018 Sch 1 Part 1 para 1 and an appropriate policy document would be needed. The ICO has no
survey-specific guidance (UNVERIFIED 2).

### 6.3 «Anonymous» in UK data protection law (ICO)

- «In data protection law, anonymous information is data that does not relate to an identified or identifiable person (ie
  data that is not personal data). Data protection law does not apply to anonymous information.» (ICO, «Introduction to
  anonymisation»).
- «We use the term ‘effective anonymisation’ to mean the technical and organisational measures you need to ensure that the
  status of the data meets the legal threshold for anonymisation under UK GDPR. Any information that does not meet this
  threshold is not anonymous information, and you must treat it as personal data.» (same page).
- «Yes. For the purposes of data protection law, applying anonymisation techniques to turn personal data into anonymous
  information counts as processing personal data.» (same page).
- «Identifiability exists on a spectrum. When assessing whether someone is identifiable, you should take account of the
  “means reasonably likely to be used to enable identification”.» · «A person can be identifiable even if you do not know
  their name.» · «Even if you do not intend to take action about a person, the fact that they can be singled out may allow
  you, or others, to do so. This means the person is still identifiable.» · «Applying a “motivated intruder” test is a good
  starting point to consider identifiability risk.» (ICO, «How do we ensure anonymisation is effective?»).

**Consequence for product texts:** the stored responses, the invitation tokens and the comment threads are processing of
personal data, however strong the structural anonymity is. Aggregates that pass the k floor may be anonymous information
for the employer who reads them. Write «no answer can be traced to a person» (what the product guarantees), not «we
process no personal data».

### 6.4 DPIA

- UK GDPR art. 35(1), as quoted by the ICO: «Where a type of processing in particular using new technologies, and taking
  into account the nature, scope, context and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the
  rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall, prior to the processing, carry out an assessment of the
  impact of the envisaged processing operations on the protection of personal data.»
- **Employees can count as vulnerable:** «One group who may count as vulnerable in this sense are employees. The European
  guidelines on DPIAs (WP248) explain why employees could be considered vulnerable data subjects where a power imbalance
  means they cannot easily consent or object to the processing of their data by an employer.» · «If you think your
  processing will involve vulnerable individuals, then a DPIA will be required should any of the other criteria, or
  operations on our list, be engaged.» · «In most cases, a combination of two of these factors indicates the need for a
  DPIA. However, this is not a strict rule.» · «If in any doubt, we would always recommend that you do a DPIA to ensure
  compliance and encourage best practice.» (ICO, «When do we need to do a DPIA?»). The nine WP29 criteria the ICO lists
  include «Evaluation or scoring», «Sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature» and «Data concerning vulnerable data
  subjects».
- On health data: «You should carry out a DPIA given the sensitive and potentially intrusive nature of processing workers’
  health information.» (ICO, «Data protection and workers' health information»).
- On monitoring: «You should carry out a DPIA even if there is no specific high risk as it is a flexible and scalable tool
  which can assist your decision-making. If you decide to proceed without carrying out a DPIA, you should document your
  decision.» (ICO, «Data protection and monitoring workers»).

**Assessment:** a psychosocial survey of employees meets at least «vulnerable data subjects» and arguably «data of a highly
personal nature» or «evaluation». A UK employer should be told that a DPIA is expected, and the product can supply a DPIA
template as processor assistance (UK GDPR art. 28). The ICO has no survey-specific statement (UNVERIFIED 2).

### 6.5 Complaints (DPA 2018 s.164A) in the product

The duty falls on the **controller**, the employer. **Assessment:** an Empvibe employer needs a way to receive a data
protection complaint «which can be completed electronically and by other means», and to acknowledge it within 30 days.
Orgpuls AS, as processor, assists. A survey-page link to the employer's complaint route meets s.164A(2) for the

### 6.7 UK representative (UK GDPR art. 27)

- Art. 3(2): «This Regulation applies to the relevant processing of personal data of data subjects who are in the United
  Kingdom by a controller or processor not established in the United Kingdom, where the processing activities are related
  to: (a) the offering of goods or services, irrespective of whether a payment of the data subject is required, to such
  data subjects in the United Kingdom; or (b) the monitoring of their behaviour as far as their behaviour takes place
  within the United Kingdom.»
- Art. 27(1): «Where Article 3(2) applies, the controller or the processor shall designate in writing a representative in
  the United Kingdom.» Art. 27(2): «The obligation laid down in paragraph 1 of this Article shall not apply to: (a)
  processing which is occasional, does not include, on a large scale, processing of special categories of data … and is
  unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons …; or (b) a public authority or body.» Art.
  27(4): the representative is «to be addressed in addition to or instead of the controller or the processor by, in
  particular, the Commission and data subjects, on all issues related to processing».
- DPA 2018 s.207(1A): «In the case of the processing of personal data to which Part 2 (the UK GDPR) applies, it applies to
  the types of such processing to which the UK GDPR applies by virtue of Article 3 of the UK GDPR.» s.207(7) lists what
  counts as an establishment in the UK, including «(d) a person … who maintains, and carries on activities through, an
  office, branch or agency or other stable arrangements in the United Kingdom».

**Assessment:** Orgpuls AS has no UK establishment. Two points are open. (1) Whether art. 3(2) catches a non-UK processor
whose service is offered to UK employers rather than «to such data subjects». (2) If it does, whether a recurring survey
service could be «occasional» under art. 27(2)(a). It is unlikely that it could. Separately, Orgpuls AS is a controller
for its own UK customer-contact and billing data, offered to UK businesses, and the same art. 3(2) question arises there.
ICO guidance on UK representatives was not located on the current ico.org.uk structure (UNVERIFIED 4). This remains Tor's
decision (X-111 «Open»).

  relation to a supply of services if— (a) the person carries on a business, and (b) the services are not received by the
  person wholly for private purposes, whether or not the services are received in the course of business.»
- **Reverse charge, VATA 1994 s.8(1)–(2):** «Where services are supplied by a person who belongs in a country other than
  the United Kingdom in circumstances in which this subsection applies, this Act has effect as if (instead of there being a
  supply of the services by that person)— (a) there were a supply of the services by the recipient in the United Kingdom in
  the course or furtherance of a business carried on by the recipient, and (b) that supply were a taxable supply.» ·
  «Subsection (1) above applies if— (a) the recipient is a relevant business person who belongs in the United Kingdom, and
  (b) the place of supply of the services is inside the United Kingdom …».
- **VAT Notice 741A** (last updated 29 September 2022):
  - 6.3: «The B2B general rule for supplies of services is that the supply is made where the customer belongs.»
  - 5.1: «The reverse charge applies where: the place of supply is the UK; the supplier belongs outside the UK; you belong in
    the UK; the supply is not exempt …; for supplies not within the general rule, you’re VAT registered in the UK» · «The
    reverse charge applies to almost all B2B supplies of services except exempt supplies.» · «Where it applies to services
    which you receive, you, the customer, must act as if you are both the supplier and the recipient of the services. It
    applies if your supplier belongs outside the UK even if they have a UK VAT registration number.»
  - 5.6: «If you belong in the UK and receive supplies of any B2B general rule services (read section 6) and your supplier
    belongs outside the UK, the reverse charge applies …».
  - **2.6, the supplier's side:** «If you are not established in the UK, there is no registration threshold for taxable
    supplies of services.» · «This means that if you make any supplies of services taxable in the UK, to which the reverse
    charge (read section 5) does not apply, you must register for VAT here and account for any UK VAT to HMRC. **If you only
    make supplies of services in the UK to which the reverse charge does apply you are not entitled to register for VAT in
    the UK.**»
  - 5.7: a UK customer that is not VAT-registered still reverse-charges, and «the value of those supplies must be added to
    the value of your own taxable supplies when determining whether you should be registered for UK VAT.»
  - 2.4 (who is B2B): «Unless you have information that suggests the service is wholly for private use, you may presume
    that your customer is in business if they provide you with their VAT number.» 6.3: «Where a customer is unable to
    provide a VAT number, or other evidence to clearly demonstrate business activities, the supply should be treated as a
    B2C transaction.»
  - 14 (B2C): «Where you supply electronically supplied telecommunications and broadcasting services, your place of supply
    will be where the consumer is located.»

**Assessment:** a subscription sold by Orgpuls AS to a UK employer is a B2B general rule service. The place of supply is
the UK, and the customer accounts for UK VAT by reverse charge. Orgpuls AS is «not entitled to register» if it makes only
such supplies. The risk is a customer with no business activities and no evidence of them. That supply would be B2C, so a
UK registration with no threshold would follow. The product should collect a UK VAT number, or evidence of business
activity, at checkout. What the invoice must say, and the Norwegian MVA side, were not researched (UNVERIFIED 12).

**How a product text should cite it:** «Reverse charge: the customer accounts for UK VAT (VATA 1994 s.8; HMRC VAT Notice
741A, sections 5 and 6.3).»

## 9. What would make a product text misleading in the UK

The legal hook for B2B marketing is BPR 2008 reg 3: «Advertising which is misleading is prohibited.» Advertising is
misleading which «in any way, including its presentation, deceives or is likely to deceive the traders to whom it is
addressed or whom it reaches; and by reason of its deceptive nature, is likely to affect their economic behaviour». Reg
3(3)–(5) name «results to be expected from use of the product» and the advertiser's «qualifications» and «awards and
distinctions». Inside the product, a wrong legal statement is also a documentation risk for the customer.
